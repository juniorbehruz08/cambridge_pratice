Research using twins

To biomedical researchers all over the world, twins offer a precious opportunity to untangle the influence of genes and the environment - of nature and nurture. Because identical twins come from a single fertilized egg that splits into two, they share virtually the same genetic code. Any differences between them - one twin having younger looking skin, for example - must be due to environmental factors such as less time spent in the sun.

Alternatively, by comparing the experiences of identical twins with those of fraternal twins, who come from separate eggs and share on average half their DNA, researchers can quantify the extent to which our genes affect our lives. If identical twins are more similar to each other with respect to an ailment than fraternal twins are, then vulnerability to the disease must be rooted at least in part in heredity.

These two lines of research - studying the differences between identical twins to pinpoint the influence of environment, and comparing identical twins with fraternal ones to measure inheritance - have been crucial to understanding the interplay of nature and nurture in determining personalities, behaviour and vulnerability to disease.

The idea of using twins to measure heredity dates back to 1875, when the English scientist Francis Galton first suggested the approach and coined the phrase "nature and nurture". But twin studies took a surprising twist in the 1980s, with studies of identical twins who had been separated at birth and reunited as adults. Over two decades, 137 sets of twins visited Thomas Bouchard's lab in what became known as the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart. Numerous tests were carried out, and each twin was asked more than 15,000 questions.

Bouchard and his colleagues used this mountain of data to identify how far twins were affected by their genetic makeup. Their key tool was a statistical concept called heritability. In broad terms, the heritability of a trait measures the extent to which differences among members of a population can be explained by differences in their genetics. Wherever Bouchard and other scientists looked, they found the invisible hand of genetic influence helping to shape our lives.

Lately, however, twin studies have helped lead scientists to a radical new conclusion: nature and nurture are not the only elemental forces at work. According to the recent field of epigenetics, a third factor is also in play. In some cases it serves as a bridge between the environment and our genes, and in others it operates on its own to shape who we are.

Epigenetic processes are chemical reactions tied to neither nature nor nurture but representing what researchers have called a "third component". These reactions influence how our genetic code is expressed: how each gene is strengthened or weakened, even turned on or off, to build our bones, brains and all the other parts of our bodies.

If our DNA is imagined as an immense piano keyboard and our genes as the keys, each key symbolizing a segment of DNA responsible for a particular note or trait, then epigenetic processes determine when and how each key can be struck, changing the tune being played.

One way epigenetics is revolutionizing biology is by revealing a mechanism by which the environment directly impacts genes. Studies of animals have shown that when a rat experiences stress during pregnancy, it can cause epigenetic changes in a fetus that lead to behavioural problems as the rodent grows up. Other epigenetic processes appear to occur randomly, while others are normal, such as those that guide embryonic cells as they become heart, brain or liver cells.

Geneticist Danielle Reed has worked with many twins and thought deeply about what twin studies have taught us. She says it is clear that much of what twins share is hardwired, but also that other things differ. In her view, epigenetics is the origin of many of those differences.

Reed credits Thomas Bouchard's work for today's surge in twin studies. She says he was the trailblazer, reminding us that 50 years ago conditions such as heart disease were thought to be caused entirely by lifestyle and schizophrenia was blamed on poor mothering. Twin studies have allowed researchers to be more reflective about what people are born with and what is caused by experience.

Reed adds that the latest work in epigenetics promises to take understanding even further. "Nature writes some things in pencil and some things in pen," she says. "Things written in pen you can't change. That's DNA. But things written in pencil you can. That's epigenetics. Now that we're actually able to look at the DNA and see where the pencil writings are, it's sort of a whole new world."

An Introduction to Film Sound

Though we might think of film as an essentially visual experience, we cannot afford to underestimate the importance of film sound. A meaningful sound track is often as complicated as the image on the screen and is ultimately just as much the responsibility of the director. The entire sound track consists of three essential ingredients: the human voice, sound effects and music. These tracks must be mixed and balanced to produce the necessary emphases which create desired effects.

Dialogue serves to tell the story and expresses feelings and motivations of characters. Often, in film characterization, the audience perceives little or no difference between the character and the actor. For example, Humphrey Bogart is the character Sam Spade; film personality and life personality seem to merge. Perhaps this is because the texture of a performer's voice supplies an element of character. When voice textures fit the performer's appearance and gestures, a whole and realistic persona emerges.

How dialogue is used and how much dialogue is used vary widely among films. In the science-fiction film 2001, little dialogue is evident, and most of it is banal and of little intrinsic interest. In this way, the film-maker portrays what Thomas Sobochack and Vivian Sobochack call the inadequacy of human responses when compared with magnificent technology and the visual beauties of the universe.

The comedy Bringing Up Baby, on the other hand, presents practically non-stop dialogue delivered at breakneck speed. This underscores not only the dizzy quality of the character played by Katharine Hepburn, but also the absurdity and humour of the film itself. The audience is bounced from gag to gag and conversation to conversation, with no time for reflection. This film presents pure escapism, largely because of its frenetic dialogue.

Synchronous sound effects are those sounds which are synchronized or matched with what is viewed. If the film portrays a character playing the piano, the sounds of the piano are projected. Synchronous sounds contribute to the realism of film and help create atmosphere. The click of a door being opened may simply convince the audience that the image is real. But if the click is part of an ominous action, such as a burglary, the sound mixer may call attention to it by increasing the volume, engaging the audience in suspense.

Asynchronous sound effects are not matched with a visible source of sound on screen. Such sounds provide an appropriate emotional nuance and may add to realism. A film-maker might include the background sound of an ambulance siren while the foreground image portrays an arguing couple. The siren underscores the psychic injury incurred in the argument, while also acknowledging the film's city setting.

Background music in films has become so ubiquitous that its absence is noticeable. We are aware that it adds emotion and rhythm. Usually not meant to be noticed, it provides a tone or emotional attitude toward the story and characters. It often foreshadows a change in mood; dissonant music may indicate an approaching menace or disaster. Background music may also aid viewer understanding by linking scenes, repeating a theme associated with a character or situation to remind the audience of important motifs or ideas.

Film sound comprises conventions and innovations. We expect accelerating music during car chases and creaky doors in horror films. Yet sound is often brilliantly conceived, and its effects are often subtle, noted only by our subconscious minds. We need to foster an awareness of film sound as well as film space to truly appreciate the modern film, an art form that sprang to life during the twentieth century.

This Marvellous Invention

A

Of all mankind's manifold creations, language must take pride of place. Other inventions - the wheel, agriculture, sliced bread - may have transformed our material existence, but the advent of language is what made us human. Compared to language, all other inventions pale in significance, since everything we have ever achieved depends on language and originates from it. Without language, we could never have embarked on our ascent to unparalleled power over other animals and nature itself.

B

But language is foremost not just because it came first. It is a tool of extraordinary sophistication, based on an idea of ingenious simplicity: composing out of twenty-five or thirty sounds an infinite variety of expressions which allow us to disclose to others the secrets of our mind. This was how, in 1660, the French grammarians of the Port-Royal abbey distilled the essence of language, and no one since has celebrated more eloquently the magnitude of its achievement.

C

Even so, there is one flaw in these hymns of praise: homage to language's unique accomplishment conceals a simple yet critical incongruity. Language is mankind's greatest invention, except that it was never invented. This paradox is central to our fascination with language and holds many of its secrets.

D

Language often seems so skilfully drafted that one can hardly imagine it as anything other than the perfected handiwork of a master craftsman. How else could this instrument make so much out of barely three dozen morsels of sound? In themselves, these configurations of mouth, lips and so on are only random noises with no meaning or power. But run them through the language machine, let it arrange them in special orders, and there is nothing that these meaningless streams of air cannot do, from sighing boredom to unravelling the fundamental order of the universe.

E

The most extraordinary thing about language is that one does not have to be a genius to set its wheels in motion. The language machine allows almost everybody, from pre-modern foragers to post-modern philosophers, to tie these meaningless sounds together into an infinite variety of subtle senses, apparently without exertion. Yet this deceptive ease makes language a victim of its own success: in everyday life its triumphs are usually taken for granted, and one rarely stops to think about the resourcefulness that must have gone into making it tick.

F

Often, only the estrangement of foreign tongues, with their exotic features, brings home the wonder of language's design. Some languages can build words of breath-breaking length and express in one word what English needs a whole sentence to say. Turkish offers examples of huge words whose components cannot stand alone.

Sumerian, spoken on the banks of the Euphrates some 5,000 years ago by the people who invented writing and enabled the documentation of history, shows the opposite: thrifty compactness. A Sumerian word may be made of slots, each corresponding to a portion of meaning. This sleek design allows single sounds to convey information, and even the absence of a sound can express something specific. The technology is so fine-tuned that a non-sound, carefully placed, has a function.
