The Story of Silk

The history of the world's most luxurious fabric, from ancient China to the present day

Silk is a fine, smooth material produced from the cocoons - soft protective shells - that are made by mulberry silkworms. Legend has it that Lei Tzu, wife of the Yellow Emperor, ruler of China in about 3000 BC, discovered silkworms. One account says that while taking a walk in her husband's gardens, she noticed that silkworms were responsible for the destruction of several mulberry trees. She collected some cocoons and sat down to rest. While she was sipping tea, one cocoon landed in the hot drink and started to unravel into a fine thread.

Lei Tzu found that she could wind this thread around her fingers. Subsequently, she persuaded her husband to allow her to rear silkworms on a grove of mulberry trees. She also devised a special reel to draw the fibres from the cocoon into a single thread so that they would be strong enough to be woven into fabric. Although the details of the legend are uncertain, silk cultivation has existed in China for several millennia.

Originally, silkworm farming was restricted to women, who were responsible for growing, harvesting and weaving. Silk quickly grew into a symbol of status and, at first, only royalty were allowed to wear clothes made of silk. The rules were gradually relaxed, and by the Qing Dynasty even the lowest castes could wear silk. During the Han Dynasty, silk became so prized that it was used as a unit of currency. Officials were paid their salary in silk and farmers paid taxes in grain and silk. Silk was also used as diplomatic gifts by the emperor.

Fishing lines, bowstrings, musical instruments and paper were all made using silk. The earliest indication of silk paper being used was discovered in the tomb of a noble who is estimated to have died around 168 AD.

Demand for this exotic fabric eventually created the lucrative trade route now known as the Silk Road, taking silk westward and bringing gold, silver and wool to the East. The route stretched over 6,000 kilometres from eastern China to the Mediterranean Sea, following the Great Wall of China, climbing the Pamir mountain range, crossing modern-day Afghanistan and continuing to the Middle East, with a major trading market in Damascus. From there, merchandise was shipped across the Mediterranean Sea. Few merchants travelled the entire route; goods were handled mostly by a series of middlemen.

With the mulberry silkworm native to China, the country was the world's sole producer of silk for many hundreds of years. The secret eventually reached the rest of the world via the Byzantine Empire. According to another legend, monks working for Emperor Justinian carried silkworm eggs to Constantinople in 550 AD, hidden inside hollow bamboo walking canes. The Byzantines were also secretive, and for many centuries weaving and trading silk fabric remained a strict imperial monopoly.

In the seventh century, the Arabs conquered Persia, capturing its magnificent silks. Silk production then spread through Africa, Sicily and Spain. Andalusia in southern Spain was Europe's main silk-producing centre in the tenth century. By the thirteenth century, Italy had become Europe's leader in silk production and export. Venetian merchants traded extensively in silk and encouraged silk growers to settle in Italy. Even now, silk processed in Como in northern Italy has a strong reputation.

The nineteenth century and industrialisation saw the downfall of the European silk industry. Cheaper Japanese silk, helped by the opening of the Suez Canal, was one factor driving the trend. In the twentieth century, new man-made fibres such as nylon began to be used in products that had traditionally used silk, such as stockings and parachutes. The two world wars also interrupted the supply of raw material from Japan and further damaged the European silk industry.

After the Second World War, Japan's silk production was restored and it remained the world's biggest producer of raw silk until the 1970s. In more recent decades, however, China has gradually recaptured its position as the world's biggest producer and exporter of raw silk and silk yarn. Today, around 125,000 metric tons of silk are produced worldwide, and almost two thirds of that production takes place in China.

Great Migrations

Animal migration, however it is defined, is far more than just the movement of animals. It can loosely be described as travel that takes place at regular intervals, often in an annual cycle, may involve many members of a species, and is rewarded only after a long journey. It suggests inherited instinct.

The biologist Hugh Dingle has identified five characteristics that apply, in varying degrees and combinations, to all migrations. They are prolonged movements that carry animals outside familiar habitats; they tend to be linear, not zigzagging; they involve special behaviours concerning preparation, such as overfeeding, and arrival; they demand special allocations of energy. And one more: migrating animals maintain an intense attentiveness to the greater mission, which keeps them undistracted by temptations and undeterred by challenges that would turn other animals aside.

An arctic tern, on its 20,000 km flight from the extreme south of South America to the Arctic Circle, will take no notice of a smelly herring offered from a bird-watcher's boat along the way. While local gulls will dive for such handouts, the tern flies on. It resists distraction because it is driven by an instinctive sense of larger purpose. The bird senses that it can eat, rest and mate later. Right now it is focused on the journey. Reaching gravelly coastline in the Arctic will serve its larger evolutionary purpose: finding the right place and circumstances in which to hatch and rear offspring.

But migration is complex, and biologists define it differently depending on what sorts of animals they study. Joel Berger, who works on the American pronghorn and other large terrestrial mammals, prefers a practical definition: movements from one seasonal home area to another and back again. Generally this movement is to seek resources that are not available within a single area year-round.

Daily vertical movements by zooplankton in the ocean, upward by night to seek food and downward by day to escape predators, can also be considered migration. So can the movement of aphids when, having depleted the young leaves on one food plant, their offspring fly onward to a different host plant, with no one aphid ever returning to where it started.

Dingle, an evolutionary biologist who studies insects, gives a more intricate definition. His five features allow for the fact that aphids become sensitive to blue light from the sky when it is time for takeoff and sensitive to yellow light reflected from tender young leaves when it is appropriate to land. Birds will fatten themselves with heavy feeding before a long migrational flight. Dingle argues that his definition focuses attention on what wildebeest migration shares with aphid movement, helping researchers understand how evolution has produced them all.

Human behaviour, however, is having a detrimental impact on animal migration. The pronghorn, which resembles an antelope though they are unrelated, is the fastest land mammal of the New World. One population spends summer in the mountainous Grand Teton National Park in the western USA, then follows a narrow route across a river and down onto the plains. There they wait out the frozen months, feeding mainly on sagebrush blown clear of snow.

These pronghorn are notable for the invariance of their migration route and the severity of its constriction at three bottlenecks. If they cannot pass through each bottleneck during spring migration, they cannot reach their summer grazing; if they cannot pass through again in autumn, escaping south onto the windblown plains, they are likely to die trying to overwinter in deep snow.

Pronghorn depend on distance vision and speed to keep safe from predators. They traverse high, open shoulders of land, where they can see and run. At one bottleneck, forested hills form a V, leaving a corridor of open ground only about 150 metres wide, filled with private homes. Increasing development threatens to choke off the passageway.

Conservation scientists, biologists and land managers are now working to preserve migrational behaviours, not just species and habitats. A National Forest has recognised much of the pronghorn path as a protected migration corridor. But agencies cannot control what happens on private land at a bottleneck. With other migrating species, the challenge is complicated further by greater distances, more jurisdictions, more borders and more dangers along the way. Wisdom and resoluteness will be needed to ensure that migrating species can continue their journeys.

Preface to 'How the other half thinks: Adventures in mathematical reasoning'

A

Occasionally, in difficult musical compositions, there are beautiful but easy parts, so simple a beginner could play them. So it is with mathematics. Some discoveries in advanced mathematics do not depend on specialised knowledge, not even algebra, geometry or trigonometry. Instead they may involve only a little arithmetic, such as "the sum of two odd numbers is even", and common sense. Each chapter in this book illustrates this phenomenon. Anyone can understand every step in the reasoning, so all readers can participate in a mathematical experience and appreciate the beauty and style of mathematical thinking.

B

One purpose in writing this book is to give readers who have not had the opportunity to see and enjoy real mathematics the chance to appreciate the mathematical way of thinking. I want to reveal not only fascinating discoveries but, more importantly, the reasoning behind them. In that respect, this book differs from most mathematics books for the general public. Some present the lives of colourful mathematicians. Others describe important applications. Others go into procedures but assume that the reader is adept in algebra.

C

I hope this book will help bridge the notorious gap separating the two cultures: the humanities and the sciences, or the right brain and the left brain. Mathematics is not restricted to the analytical and numerical; intuition plays a significant role. The gap can be narrowed by anyone, partly because each of us is far from using the full capacity of either side of the brain. To illustrate human potential, I cite a structural engineer who is an artist, an electrical engineer who is an opera singer, an opera singer who published mathematical research, and a mathematician who publishes short stories.

D

Other scientists have written books to explain their fields to non-scientists, but they have necessarily had to omit the mathematics, although it provides the foundation of their theories. The reader must remain a spectator rather than an involved participant, since the appropriate language for describing much of science is mathematics, whether the subject is the expanding universe, subatomic particles or chromosomes. Though the broad outline of a scientific theory can be sketched intuitively, when part of the physical universe is finally understood, its description often looks like a page in a mathematics text.

E

Still, the non-mathematical reader can go far in understanding mathematical reasoning. This book presents details that illustrate the mathematical style of thinking, involving sustained step-by-step analysis, experiments and insights. You will turn these pages much more slowly than when reading a novel or a newspaper. It may help to have a pencil and paper ready to check claims and carry out experiments.

F

As I wrote, I kept in mind two types of readers: those who enjoyed mathematics until they were turned off by an unpleasant episode, usually around fifth grade, and mathematics aficionados, who will find much that is new throughout the book. This book also serves readers who simply want to sharpen their analytical skills. Many careers, such as law and medicine, require extended, precise analysis.

G

Each chapter offers practice in following a sustained and closely argued line of thought. That mathematics can develop this skill is shown by testimonials. A physician wrote that the discipline of analytical thought processes in mathematics prepared him extremely well for medical school. A lawyer made the same point, saying that studying mathematics, and in particular theorems, helped him analyse complicated principles. I hope readers will share my delight in watching simple questions lead to remarkable solutions and theoretical discoveries find unanticipated applications.
